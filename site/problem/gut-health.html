Check before choosing

If something is severe, sudden, or getting rapidly worse, do not work through a protocol — call your local emergency number and go to an emergency department.

This page is information, not medical advice. No clinician has reviewed it, and nothing on it is a diagnosis.

When to get professional care

Stop self-treating and see a doctor promptly if you have blood in your stool or black, tarry stools, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks (especially if you're over 50 or have a family history of bowel cancer), vomiting that won't stop, a new lump or swelling in your tummy, fever, night sweats, or severe unrelenting pain, waking at night to open your bowels, or iron-deficiency anaemia (low blood iron, usually found on a blood test — it can be a sign of slow, hidden bleeding).

These are warning signs that point past 'gut health' toward something needing proper tests — no amount of fibre or fermented food substitutes for that check. This page is here to help you understand your options, not to replace a doctor's assessment.

For anything persistent, a family doctor or other qualified professional is the right first stop.

Starting pointDysbiosis / low fibre diversity

Bloated, irregular, and stuck eating the same few foods?

Is this you? Is this you? Your bloating, hard or infrequent stools and general gut grumbling seem to track with a monotonous, beige, low-plant diet, and they ease when you add more vegetables, legumes and whole grains — even if gas briefly gets worse first. You tend to eat a fairly narrow range of foods and rarely reach for a wide variety of plants.

The key insight: Your gut bacteria don't eat the food you eat — they eat the plant fibre you can't digest. The narrower and more beige your diet, the fewer of these helpful bacteria survive to make the fuel that keeps your gut wall fed, sealed and calm.

Practical next steps

Work down the list — cheapest and safest first. The tag on each step is the regulator’s current classification, not a recommendation.

  • Food Aim for 30+ different plant species per week (rotate vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds, whole grains) — diversity of fibre matters more than total grams; note this target comes from observational data (American Gut Project), not a trial
  • Supplement Add a progressive prebiotic fibre — start low and ramp to avoid gas
  • Compound Daily fermented foods (yoghurt, kefir, kimchi) — the best-evidenced way to raise diversity and lower inflammatory markers; oral butyrate is an optional adjunct with only limited human data
  • Food Cut added sugar and ultra-processed foods, which crowd out fibre and favour low-diversity communities
  • Supplement A multi-strain probiotic during the rebuild phase — evidence is modest and strain-specific
How this works

The pathway — step by step

You eat the same few beige foods, day after day

the trigger Low plant and fibre variety in the diet

This is where it starts, so there's nothing before it to blame — just the everyday pattern of a narrow diet. Fibre is the part of plants — the stringy, structural material in vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains — that your own digestive system genuinely cannot break down or absorb, so it passes all the way through to your large intestine intact. Here's the key point most people miss: there isn't one single fibre, there are many different kinds, and each type of plant carries its own mix.

That means variety matters as much as amount — eating a big bowl of white rice and bread every day gives you very little fibre and almost no diversity of it. When your meals stay monotonous and low in plants, only a small handful of fibre types ever reach the deeper parts of your gut.

Your gut's helpful bacteria run out of food and thin out

the mechanism Too few fermentable substrate — The raw material an enzyme acts on. → butyrate-producing species thin out

Because so few kinds of fibre are arriving from your narrow diet, the bacteria waiting downstream simply don't get fed. Living in your large intestine are trillions of bacteria — collectively called your gut microbiome, meaning the whole community of microbes that live in your digestive tract — and many of the helpful ones survive by eating fibre.

The way they eat it is called fermentation, which just means breaking food down without oxygen, the same basic process that turns grapes into wine; the fibre they feast on is their fermentable substrate, a plain term for 'the raw material they can actually digest.' Crucially, different bacteria prefer different fibres, so when only a couple of fibre types show up, the specialists that need the missing ones have nothing to eat.

In particular, the butyrate-producing species — the bacteria whose job is to make a specific fuel called butyrate — get starved out and dwindle in number.

A key fuel your gut makes, called butyrate, drops

the mechanism Butyrate / short-chain fatty acids fall

Because those specialist bacteria have thinned out, the substance they used to manufacture starts to run short. That substance is butyrate, one of a small family of molecules called short-chain fatty acids — think of them as tiny energy-rich packets that the bacteria release as a by-product when they ferment your fibre. In a well-fed gut, this is a beautiful trade: you hand your bacteria fibre you couldn't use, and they hand you back butyrate that your body very much can use.

But butyrate isn't something you can eat directly in any meaningful amount — you have to grow the bacteria that make it, by feeding them fibre. So when the fibre variety collapses and the butyrate-makers shrink, your butyrate and short-chain fatty acid levels fall, and the steady supply your gut has come to rely on quietly dries up.

The cells lining your gut get starved and the wall turns leaky

in the tissue Colonocytes lose their main fuel (butyrate supplies ~70% of their energy); mucus layer thins, barrier and tight junctions weaken

Because butyrate has fallen, the cells that depend on it are suddenly left short of fuel. The lining of your large intestine is built from cells called colonocytes — simply 'colon cells,' the single layer that forms the inner surface of your gut wall. Here's what makes them unusual: while most cells in your body run on sugar (glucose, the simple sugar carried in your blood), colonocytes run mainly on butyrate delivered right next door by your bacteria — it supplies roughly 70% of their energy.

So when butyrate drops, these cells are effectively going hungry in a way most of your body never is. Underfed colonocytes can't keep up their maintenance work, and two protections weaken as a result.

First, the mucus layer — a slippery protective gel that normally coats the gut wall and keeps bacteria at arm's length — grows thin; and second, the tight junctions, the microscopic 'stitching' that seals neighbouring cells together into a solid, leak-proof barrier, start to loosen, so the wall that should stay firmly shut becomes a little leaky.

You feel bloated, irregular, and mildly inflamed

the symptom Bloating, irregularity, low-grade gut inflammation

Because the mucus coat has thinned and the wall's seams have loosened, the calm, well-defended gut you're meant to have becomes irritable and less predictable. With the bacterial community disturbed and butyrate low, the muscle movements that gently march food along can lose their steady rhythm, which shows up as irregularity — hard, infrequent or unpredictable stools.

At the same time, a thinner mucus layer and looser seams let bacteria and their fragments brush up against the gut wall in a way they normally wouldn't, and your immune system — your body's built-in defence force — reacts with a persistent, low-level inflammation, meaning mild ongoing irritation and swelling in the tissue rather than a dramatic illness. The trapped gas from all this sluggish, uneven fermentation is felt as bloating — that tight, over-full feeling in your belly.

And this is exactly why adding a wider range of plants tends to help — though the extra fibre can briefly increase gas at first, as your hungry bacteria wake up and get back to work.

Deep in your large intestine lives a community of trillions of bacteria — your "gut microbiome" — and many of the helpful ones survive by feeding on plant fibre that your own body can't break down. When your diet is monotonous and low in plants, only a few kinds of fibre arrive, so the bacteria that specialise in making a fuel called butyrate slowly starve and shrink in number.

With fewer of them working, butyrate levels drop, and that matters because butyrate is the main energy source for the very cells that line your gut wall. Underfed, that lining thins its protective mucus coat and its seams loosen, which shows up as bloating, irregularity and a low simmer of gut inflammation. The encouraging part: feeding those bacteria a wider variety of plants usually rebuilds their numbers and their fuel supply.

How well established is this mechanism: Reasonably established — this rates the causal link, not how much a given fix will help you.

Starting pointStress / gut-brain axis dysregulation (IBS spectrum)

Your gut flares up exactly when life gets stressful.

Is this you? Do your flare-ups line up with deadlines, worry or bad sleep, and then quietly settle when you are on holiday? Do you swing between diarrhoea and constipation, with bloating and pain that feel far bigger than whatever you actually ate — often turning up alongside anxiety?

The key insight: Your gut has its own dense web of nerves wired straight to your brain, so when your mind is under pressure your bowel genuinely feels it — the bloating and pain are real, even when there is nothing wrong with the food you ate.

Practical next steps

Work down the list — cheapest and safest first. The tag on each step is the regulator’s current classification, not a recommendation.

  • Behaviour Daily down-regulation: 5–10 min diaphragmatic/slow breathing or vagal work before meals to shift out of sympathetic dominance
  • Behaviour Fix sleep regularity and total hours — poor sleep directly amplifies visceral hypersensitivity
  • Behaviour Don't eat while stressed or rushed; sit, slow down, chew — motility is state-dependent
  • Supplement Support the barrier while the axis is calming — modest evidence (best data are in post-infectious IBS-D)
  • Behaviour Gut-directed hypnotherapy or CBT is the highest-evidence intervention for stress-driven IBS (~70% respond; recommended by NICE and the AGA)
How this works

The pathway — step by step

Ongoing stress and bad sleep keep your body on high alert

the trigger Chronic psychological stress / poor sleep

This whole chain starts not in your stomach but in your head. When life piles on — deadlines, worry, argument, week after week of short or broken sleep — your body reads it as a chronic (meaning long-lasting, not a one-off) stress state and stays braced, as if danger might arrive at any moment. Your body cannot easily tell the difference between a physical threat and a mental one, so a stressful inbox and a genuine emergency switch on the same internal alarm.

Poor sleep makes this worse, because sleep is when your nervous system is supposed to power down and reset, and without it the alarm never fully switches off. Nothing has happened in your gut yet — but the signal that will reach it has just been set in motion.

Your brain flips on the body's main stress-alarm system

the mechanism hpa axis — The brain–adrenal stress circuit that controls cortisol. and CRF (corticotropin-releasing factor) signalling switch on

Because that sense of ongoing threat is now switched on, your brain reaches for its central stress-response system, known as the HPA axis. The name is just a chain of three body parts that pass a message down the line: the hypothalamus — The brain's control panel for hormones, hunger, temperature and stress. (a control centre deep in your brain), the pituitary (a pea-sized gland — a small organ whose job is to release chemical messengers), and the adrenal glands (two small glands sitting on top of your kidneys).

The very first messenger the hypothalamus fires off is called CRF, short for corticotropin-releasing factor — think of it as the starter pistol that tells the rest of the chain to go. These messengers are hormones, meaning chemicals that travel in your bloodstream to carry instructions from one part of the body to another. So the mental pressure from Step 1 has now been converted into a physical, chemical signal spreading through your body.

Stress chemicals make your gut's pain nerves touchy

the mechanism Cortisol + mucosal mast-cell mediators (histamine, proteases) sensitise visceral afferents

Because the alarm chain from Step 2 has fired, two things now flood your gut. First, the adrenal glands release cortisol, the body's main stress hormone, which keeps you keyed up and primes your tissues (the body's sheets of living cells) to react. Second, that stress signal pokes special immune cells sitting in your gut wall called mast cells — an immune cell is one of your body's defence units, and mast cells are little storage packets that burst open and spill their contents when provoked.

What they spill is histamine (the same irritating chemical behind an allergic itch or swelling) and proteases (which are enzymes — proteins, the body's tiny molecular machines, that here speed up chemical reactions by snipping neighbouring proteins apart). This chemical cocktail washes over your visceral afferents, the sensory nerves that carry sensation from your internal organs up to your brain, and sensitises them — meaning it lowers their trigger point so they fire off at the gentlest touch.

Your gut's warning system has just had its volume dial cranked right up.

Your gut's movement, barrier and pain wiring all go haywire

in the tissue Motility speeds up or stalls, tight junctions loosen, visceral pain nerves become hypersensitive

Now that those nerves and tissues are chemically wound up from Step 3, the physical workings of your gut start to misbehave in three ways at once. The motility — the rhythmic squeezing of muscle that pushes food along — either races ahead too fast or grinds to a stall, because the same stress chemicals that sensitise nerves also disturb this muscle timing.

The tight junctions, which are the glue-like seals between the cells lining your gut wall that normally keep the barrier snug, loosen slightly, making the lining a touch leakier and a touch more irritable. And the visceral pain nerves from the last step are now so hypersensitive that a completely normal amount of gas or stretch — something you would usually never feel — gets read as a real problem. Nothing has been damaged; the settings on your gut have simply been knocked out of balance.

You feel bloating, cramping and unpredictable bowels — with pain out of all proportion

the symptom Bloating, cramping, urgency or constipation, pain out of proportion to gas

Because your gut is now moving erratically, sealed a little loosely, and wired to over-report pain, all of that finally surfaces as how you actually feel. When the muscle races, you get diarrhoea and urgency — that sudden must-go-now feeling; when it stalls, you get constipation, which is why many people ping-pong between the two.

Because the pain nerves are turned up so high, an ordinary pocket of gas that should pass unnoticed instead lands as cramping and bloating, and the discomfort feels wildly out of proportion to the small meal or trapped air that caused it. This is the honest signature of the condition: the pain is completely real, yet it is driven by an over-sensitive alarm rather than by anything harmful in your gut — which is exactly why it eases when the stress from Step 1 finally lifts.

In irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), the machinery of the gut is physically normal — a scan or scope finds no ulcer, no damage — yet it behaves as if something is wrong. The reason is the gut-brain axis, a constant two-way conversation between your brain and the nervous system woven through your gut wall. When you are stressed or sleep-deprived, your brain sends alarm signals downward that speed up or stall the gut's muscle, loosen its lining, and turn up the volume on its pain nerves.

So ordinary amounts of gas and stretching that you would normally never notice start registering as cramping, urgency and bloating. This is why the symptoms are genuine and yet track your stress levels rather than your diet.

Starting pointFood intolerance (FODMAPs / lactose)

Same foods bloat you every time, hours later

Is this you? The same foods set you off every time — dairy, wheat, onion or garlic, beans, or sweet fruit — and if you kept a simple food-and-symptom diary you would see a clear, repeatable pattern. On a plain, low-trigger day you often feel completely fine, which is the giveaway that a specific food, not your gut in general, is the problem.

The key insight: Some everyday sugars in food never get absorbed by you — so they drag water into your gut and get eaten by your gut bacteria, and that water and gas is what bloats and cramps you a few hours after the meal.

Practical next steps

Work down the list — cheapest and safest first. The tag on each step is the regulator’s current classification, not a recommendation.

  • Food Run a dietitian-guided low-FODMAP elimination then systematic reintroduction — identify YOUR triggers, don't restrict forever (prolonged restriction itself lowers microbiome diversity)
  • Behaviour If lactose is the culprit: switch to lactose-free dairy or take lactase with dairy
  • Supplement A well-tolerated soluble fibre can improve overall tolerance and stool form
  • Supplement Targeted probiotic strains can modestly improve carbohydrate tolerance over time
How this works

The pathway — step by step

You eat a food loaded with certain hard-to-absorb sugars

the trigger High-FODMAP foods or lactose intake

It starts with a specific kind of meal. Some foods are rich in FODMAPs — a nickname for a family of short, poorly-absorbed carbohydrates (the food group we call carbs, which are just sugars and starches). The everyday examples are the milk sugar lactose in dairy, the fruit sugar fructose in things like apples and honey, and the sugars packed into wheat, onion, garlic, and beans.

On its own this is just eating — nothing has gone wrong yet — but you've now delivered a big load of these particular sugars into your gut, and what happens next depends on whether your body can absorb them.

Your gut can't absorb the sugar, so it stays inside

the mechanism Sugar not absorbed — e.g. lactase deficiency, or poorly-absorbed short-chain carbs

Because you've just taken in these sugars, your body now has to move them out of your small intestine — the long upper tube where most food is normally absorbed into your blood — and into your bloodstream. The catch is that these particular sugars are hard to absorb there.

Lactose, for example, needs an enzyme — A protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction in the body. (a tiny protein tool your body makes to break one substance into another) called lactase to split it before it can be taken up; many adults make very little lactase, so the lactose is never broken down. The FODMAPs have the same problem for a different reason — they're simply built in a shape your small intestine can't grab quickly. So instead of leaving through the gut wall like normal food, these sugars stay stuck inside the tube and travel onward undigested.

The trapped sugar pulls in water, then bacteria ferment it and make gas

in the tissue Osmotic water pulled into the lumen (fast) + colonic fermentation of the carbs (slower)

Now that the sugar is stuck inside your gut instead of being absorbed, it causes two problems. First, sugar attracts water — this is osmosis, the natural pull of water toward wherever there's more dissolved stuff — so water is drawn out of your body and into the lumen (the hollow inside of the gut tube). This happens fast.

Second, the undigested sugar keeps travelling down into your colon (your large intestine, the final stretch), where trillions of bacteria — the tiny living microbes that naturally make their home in your gut — are waiting. These bacteria treat the leftover sugar as free food and ferment it — break it down for energy — and fermentation releases gas as a by-product. This part is slower, which is why the trouble can build over several hours.

Extra water and gas stretch your gut, causing bloating, cramps, and loose stools

the symptom Gas, distension, cramping, loose stools 1–8 h after the trigger food

Because your gut is now holding both the extra water it pulled in and the gas the bacteria produced, the tube physically swells and stretches. Your gut wall is lined with stretch-sensitive nerves, so that swelling is exactly what you feel as distension (visible bloating), cramping (the squeezing ache as the gut muscles push against the pressure), and audible gurgling. The extra water also makes your stool looser and speeds it along, which is why you may get loose stools or a sudden urge to go.

All of this typically shows up one to eight hours after the trigger food — the fast water pull explains the early bloating, and the slower fermentation explains why gas and discomfort can arrive later the same day.

Certain natural sugars in food — the milk sugar lactose, and a group of short, poorly-absorbed carbohydrates nicknamed FODMAPs found in wheat, onion, garlic, beans, and some fruit — are hard for your small intestine to absorb. Because they stay inside your gut instead of passing into your blood, they do two things: they pull water into your gut like a sponge, and they become food for the bacteria living lower down, which ferment them and release gas.

The water and gas stretch your gut wall, and that stretching is what you feel as bloating, cramping, and looser stools, usually one to eight hours after eating. This isn't an allergy and it isn't dangerous — it's a plumbing-and-fermentation problem, and it eases when you eat less of the specific sugars your gut can't handle.

Starting pointSIBO / impaired motility

Bloated within an hour of eating — and fibre makes it worse

Is this you? Your belly balloons fast — within an hour of eating — and the swelling tends to build as the day goes on. The very things meant to help, fibre and probiotics, seem to make it worse, and it often started after a bout of food poisoning, gut surgery, or years on acid-blocking medication.

The key insight: SIBO isn't caused by "bad" bacteria — it's ordinary bacteria growing in the wrong place, because the slow wave that's meant to sweep your small intestine clean has stalled. That's exactly why the fibre and probiotics meant to help your gut can make it feel much worse.

Practical next steps

Work down the list — cheapest and safest first. The tag on each step is the regulator’s current classification, not a recommendation.

  • Behaviour Space meals 4–5 h apart with no snacking, and fast overnight, to restore the migrating motor complex that sweeps the small bowel clean between meals
  • Prescription only Rifaximin (± neomycin for methane/IMO) is the evidence-based antibiotic course — clinician-directed
  • Food Temporarily reduce fermentable fibre and probiotics during treatment, then reintroduce
  • Supplement Repair the small-bowel lining after clearance — limited direct evidence, best data are gastric
How this works

The pathway — step by step

Your gut's clean-up wave stalls

the trigger Weak migrating motor complex, slow transit, or low stomach acid

Your small intestine — the long, narrow tube just after your stomach where most of your food is broken down and absorbed into your blood — is meant to stay almost free of bacteria. Keeping it that way is a housekeeping wave called the migrating motor complex, a slow, sweeping muscle squeeze that travels down your gut roughly every 90 minutes between meals, pushing leftover food and stray bacteria toward the exit.

Two other things guard it: normal transit (simply the speed at which food travels through you) and stomach acid (the strong acid your stomach makes, which kills most bacteria as they come in). When any of these weakens — the sweeping wave turns sluggish, transit slows down, or acid runs low (often after years of acid-blocking medication) — that natural cleaning quietly stops working. Nothing hurts yet, but the door has been left open.

Bacteria move in and eat your food first

the mechanism Bacteria colonise the small intestine and ferment food before it's absorbed

Because that housekeeping wave has stalled and the acid gatekeeper has gone quiet, bacteria that should have been swept onward or killed now settle in and colonise your small intestine — meaning they set up home and multiply in a place they don't belong. Normally your food is absorbed here before bacteria ever get a share, but now the microbes reach it first and ferment it — fermentation is simply how bacteria break down sugars and fibre without using oxygen, the same reaction that makes bread rise or beer bubble.

So the carbohydrates from your meal — the sugars and starches that are meant to feed you — instead of quietly nourishing you become a feast for microbes sitting in the wrong location. This is precisely why the usual gut advice backfires: adding fibre and probiotics (live bacteria taken as supplements) just pours more fuel and more microbes into a stretch of gut that is already overcrowded.

All that feeding makes gas that inflates your gut

in the tissue Excess hydrogen / methane / hydrogen sulfide gas and distension

Fermentation doesn't only break food down — it gives off gas as a by-product, the same way a fizzy drink releases bubbles once you open it. Because so much of your meal is now being fermented high up in the small intestine, that gas is being produced exactly where there's no room for it. The three main gases are hydrogen, methane (the gas certain bacteria make by mopping up hydrogen — the same gas that burns on a kitchen stove), and hydrogen sulfide (the one that smells of rotten eggs).

All this gas inflates the thin, stretchy wall of your small intestine, causing distension — just a plain word for the gut wall physically stretching and swelling outward, like a balloon slowly filling with air.

You bloat fast after eating

the symptom Bloating within 30–90 min of eating, belching, irregularity (methane → constipation)

Because the gas builds up so quickly — right as your meal arrives and the bacteria start feeding — you feel bloated fast, usually within 30 to 90 minutes of eating, rather than the slow, mild fullness ordinary digestion gives. The stretched gut wall is packed with nerves, so that distension registers as visible swelling, tightness, and pressure that tends to worsen through the day as one meal stacks on the next.

Some of the trapped gas escapes upward as belching (ordinary burping). And methane has one extra effect worth knowing: it actually slows the muscle contractions of your gut, so people whose bacteria produce a lot of it lean toward constipation (infrequent, hard-to-pass stools) rather than the loose, urgent stools that the other gases tend to bring.

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, or SIBO, is what happens when bacteria that mostly belong lower down in your large intestine build up in the small intestine instead. The usual root cause isn't the bacteria themselves but a slowdown in the gut's natural cleaning and movement — which is why it so often follows food poisoning (which can damage the nerves that drive the cleaning wave), gut surgery, or long-term acid-blocking pills.

Because the overgrowth ferments your food before you can absorb it, the tell-tale sign is fast bloating after meals, plus the counterintuitive twist that fibre and probiotics make things worse. Diagnosis leans on a breath test that measures the gas you exhale, but it's imprecise and the condition is over-diagnosed, so the overall pattern of symptoms matters as much as any single result.

Is this you? You get loose, urgent stools — often first thing in the morning or soon after a meal — that you may have carried for years as "IBS-D." It's especially worth a look if this started after your gallbladder was removed or after Crohn's disease or surgery affecting the last part of your small intestine, and if your gut tends to settle within days on a "bile-acid binder" medicine.

The key insight: Your body normally recycles the "soap" it uses to digest fat — but if the recycling gate is broken, that soap floods your colon and flushes it out as watery, urgent diarrhoea.

How this works

The pathway — step by step

The last stretch of your small intestine stops recycling your digestive 'soap'

the trigger Ileum fails to reabsorb bile acids (idiopathic, post-cholecystectomy, or terminal-ileal Crohn's/resection)

To digest fat, your liver makes bile acids — think of them as a natural detergent, or soap, that breaks greasy food into tiny droplets your gut can absorb. Normally the ileum, which is the final section of your small intestine (the long, narrow tube where most digestion happens), acts as a recycling gate: it soaks almost all of these bile acids back up so your body can use them again.

In this condition that gate stops working properly, so the bile acids don't get reabsorbed. Sometimes there's no obvious reason (idiopathic simply means 'cause unknown'); sometimes it follows a cholecystectomy, the surgery that removes your gallbladder (the small pouch that stores bile); and sometimes it follows Crohn's disease — a condition that inflames and damages the gut — or surgery that removed that last stretch of ileum. Whatever the trigger, the recycling step fails at the source.

The leftover soap pours downstream into your large intestine

the mechanism Excess bile acids spill unabsorbed into the colon

Because your ileum didn't soak the bile acids back up, they don't just vanish — they keep travelling down the tube. Any digestive juice that isn't reabsorbed simply flows onward into the next section, which is your colon, also called the large intestine (the wider final stretch of gut where stool is normally firmed up before you pass it). So an unusually large amount of that detergent-like fluid now spills, unabsorbed, straight into a place that was never built to handle it.

Your colon is used to seeing only tiny leftover traces of bile acid, not a flood of it. That mismatch is what sets up the next step.

The soap makes your large intestine leak water and rush its contents along

in the tissue Bile acids drive colonic secretion of water and electrolytes and accelerate motility

Now that all this detergent is sitting in your colon, it irritates the colon's lining and changes how it behaves. First, it triggers secretion — meaning the wall of the colon actively pushes water and electrolytes (dissolved salts like sodium and chloride that your body uses to move water around) out into the gut instead of drawing them back in. Second, it speeds up motility, which is the muscular squeezing that moves everything through your gut, so the contents are hurried along before there's time to firm them up.

In plain terms, the bile acids turn your colon into a leaky, fast-moving tube: extra water flooding in and no time to reabsorb it. This is the direct engine behind the symptoms you feel.

You get sudden, watery, urgent diarrhoea — classically in the morning or after meals

the symptom Urgent, watery diarrhoea — often first thing in the morning or straight after eating — sometimes with pale greasy stool and bloating

All that extra water pouring into a fast-moving colon means the stool arrives loose and watery, and because it's rushing through, it comes with a strong, sudden urgency — the feeling you have to go right now. It's classically worst first thing in the morning, when bile that pooled in your gut overnight suddenly gets moving, or straight after eating, because a meal signals your body to release a fresh surge of bile.

On top of the diarrhoea, so much bile acid is now being lost downstream instead of being recycled that there can sometimes be too little left circulating to fully break down the fat in your food; that undigested fat then shows up as pale, greasy stool that floats and is hard to flush, often alongside bloating — a tight, gassy fullness in your belly.

None of this means your gut is 'weak' or that you're doing something wrong; it's simply the predictable end-result of digestive soap ending up in the wrong place, which is also why soaking it up with a bile-acid binder can settle things so quickly.

Bile acids are detergent-like molecules your liver makes to help you digest fat, and normally the last part of your small intestine soaks almost all of them back up so they can be reused. In bile-acid malabsorption, that soaking-up step fails — sometimes with no clear cause, sometimes after gallbladder removal, and sometimes after Crohn's disease or surgery has damaged or removed that stretch of gut.

The leftover bile acids pour into the colon (your large intestine), where they pull in water and speed everything up, producing urgent, watery diarrhoea. The classic clue is that it hits in the morning or right after eating, and it often eases within days once a bile-acid binder soaks up the excess. Many people live with it for years under an "IBS-D" label before the real cause is spotted.

How this works

The pathway — step by step

Common medicines and habits enter the picture

the trigger Acid blockers (PPIs), repeated antibiotics, NSAIDs, alcohol, ultra-processed diet

This all begins with things that are extremely ordinary — so ordinary you might not suspect them. PPIs (short for proton pump inhibitors, the family of drugs like omeprazole taken daily for heartburn) switch off much of your stomach's acid production. Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria to clear an infection. NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory — Something that reduces inflammation. drugs, such as ibuprofen) are the everyday painkillers you take for aches and inflammation.

Add regular alcohol and an ultra-processed diet — meaning packaged, factory-made foods that are low in fibre, the plant roughage your gut bacteria feed on — and you have the five most common disruptors. None of these is doing anything unusual; the point is simply that each one has entered your routine, setting the next step in motion.

Your gut's built-in defences get worn down

the mechanism Gastric acid barrier lowered, microbiome depleted, mucosal lining injured

Because those things are now in your system regularly, they start wearing down the gut's natural defences — and each one attacks a different defence.

Your PPIs lower the gastric acid barrier: 'gastric' just means stomach, and the strong acid your stomach normally makes is a barrier because it kills most bacteria you swallow before they can travel further down — so with less acid, that protective screen drops. Antibiotics, meanwhile, don't only kill the bacteria causing your infection; they also deplete your microbiome, the huge community of helpful bacteria living in your gut that helps you digest food and hold harmful microbes in check.

And NSAIDs, alcohol, and processed food injure the mucosal lining — the delicate, slippery inner skin of your gut wall ('mucosal' refers to that moist protective surface). So in one stroke your acid defence is lowered, your good bacteria are thinned out, and your protective lining is damaged.

The gut's balance and its wall are now compromised

in the tissue Dysbiosis + compromised, more permeable gut barrier

With those three defences worn down, the gut tips into two linked problems. The first is dysbiosis — a word meaning your microbiome has fallen out of healthy balance, so the helpful bacteria are outnumbered and less-friendly ones take over the space they left behind. This happens precisely because your good bacteria were depleted and the acid that used to hold newcomers back was lowered.

The second is a more permeable gut barrier: 'permeable' means leaky, letting things pass through that normally shouldn't. Your gut wall is supposed to be a tight, selective border — letting nutrients in while keeping bacteria and waste sealed inside the tube. Because the mucosal lining was injured in the previous step, that border no longer seals as tightly, so it becomes compromised and leakier. A gut that is both bacterially unbalanced and physically leakier is a gut whose whole defensive system is now compromised.

You feel it as the everyday gut symptoms

the symptom Bloating, altered bowel habits, reflux, slow recovery after antibiotics

Once the gut is unbalanced and leakier, that internal disruption finally surfaces as symptoms you can feel. When the wrong mix of bacteria ferments your food — 'ferments' meaning they break it down and give off gas as they do — they produce extra gas, which is why you get bloating, that tight, swollen, full feeling in your belly.

That same bacterial imbalance changes how quickly food is pushed through your system, giving altered bowel habits, meaning a new pattern of looser or harder, more or less frequent trips to the toilet than is normal for you. Reflux — the burning feeling of stomach contents rising back up into your chest or throat — comes from the same disruptors: alcohol and large, heavy processed meals loosen the ring of muscle that normally seals the top of your stomach and irritate the tube above it, so what should stay down splashes upward instead.

And because your protective bacteria were depleted, they take time to regrow, which is why you notice slow recovery after antibiotics — your gut simply feels off for weeks after a course. Each of these is the visible echo of the damage done further up the chain.

Your gut relies on three defences working together: stomach acid that kills most of the germs in what you swallow, a dense community of helpful bacteria, and an intact lining that keeps the contents of your gut where they belong. Certain very common medicines and habits — acid-suppressing pills, antibiotics, anti-inflammatory painkillers, alcohol, and a diet of processed food — each chip away at one or more of these defences.

Weaken them and the gut's bacterial balance shifts and its lining becomes leakier, which shows up as bloating, changing bowel habits, reflux, or a gut that is slow to bounce back. The reassuring part is that the cause here is often identifiable and, with your doctor, sometimes adjustable — unlike a hidden disease.
