# Creatine page: what needs a person to approve it

Everything on the creatine page today is either a quotation from a stored source, a count of stored
records, fixed RNAWiki wording, or an honest statement that something is missing. Nothing on it has
been signed off as a reviewed conclusion, and the page says so wherever that applies.

This is the list of sentences a reviewer needs to read and either approve, change or reject. It is
written for a person, not for a database. No record identifiers, no field names, no code.

For each one: what the page says now, where it comes from, what kind of evidence that is, who was
studied, what was found, what the study cannot settle, and a proposed shorter wording where the
current sentence is too long for a beginner.

---

## 1. What creatine does in the body

**The page says:** Creatine is absorbed intact and pumped into muscle cells by a dedicated
transporter. Inside, an enzyme sticks a phosphate onto it, making a small rechargeable battery.
During a maximal effort the battery hands its phosphate to spent energy molecules, keeping the cell
going for several seconds longer than it otherwise would.

**Where it comes from:** the plain explanation a person wrote into the curated record, built from
Harris 1992 and Greenhaff 1994.

**Kind of evidence:** measurements taken inside human muscle. Muscle samples before and after.

**Who was studied:** 17 healthy adults in the first study; 8 in the second.

**What was found:** total creatine in muscle rose, by as much as half in some people, and rose most
in the people who started lowest. The refill rate of the phosphate store rose by about a third, but
only in the five of eight people whose muscle actually took the creatine up.

**What it cannot settle:** it shows creatine reaches muscle and changes a chemical store there. It
does not show that anything a person does gets better.

**Proposed wording:** approve as it stands. Three short sentences, each one idea, and the claim
stops at the muscle rather than reaching for a benefit.

**What approval would change:** the page would label this as reviewed rather than as written into
the record.

## 2. Why people take it

**The page says:** Taken for strength and power output in short, hard efforts.

**Where it comes from:** the recorded use statement on the curated record.

**Kind of evidence:** none. This is a statement about why people buy it, not about what it does.

**Who was studied:** nobody. It describes a market, not a result.

**What it cannot settle:** everything. A reader could easily take it as a claim that creatine works
for those things.

**Proposed wording:** _Sold and taken for strength and power in short, hard efforts. That is what
people use it for, not a finding._

**Why change it:** the current sentence sits directly under the heading "Why people take it", which
helps, but on its own it reads like a claim. The proposed version says what kind of statement it is.

## 3. The strongest human result

**The page says:** Twelve weeks of training with creatine produced greater fat-free mass, bench
press, squat and fibre cross-sectional area than training with placebo in trained men.

**Where it comes from:** Volek 1999.

**Kind of evidence:** a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. The strongest design
here, and the only one on this page that compares creatine against a dummy treatment while both
groups trained.

**Who was studied:** 19 men who already lifted weights regularly, over 12 weeks.

**What was found:** lean mass rose about 6% against about 3% on the dummy treatment. Bench press
rose about 24% against 16%, squat about 32% against 24%. The authors put the difference down to the
creatine group doing more work in each session rather than to a direct muscle-building effect.

**What it cannot settle:** 19 people is small. They were already trained men, so it says nothing
about untrained people, women or older adults. Twelve weeks says nothing about a year.

**Proposed wording:** _In 19 trained men over 12 weeks, adding creatine to weight training produced
more lean mass and more strength than training alone. The people were few, all male, and already
training._

**Why change it:** the current sentence names four outcomes at once and leaves the number of people
and their kind to a second glance. The proposed version puts who was studied in the same breath as
the result, which is the thing beginners most often miss.

## 4. The limit that matters most

**The page says:** Animal-to-human translation in ALS: a promising survival gain in the transgenic
mouse did not appear in 175 patients.

**Where it comes from:** Groeneveld 2003.

**Kind of evidence:** a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with a stopping rule.

**Who was studied:** 175 people with motor neurone disease, also called ALS.

**What was found:** survival at one year was 70% on creatine and 68% on the dummy treatment. The
trial stopped when it became clear there was no difference to find.

**What it cannot settle:** this is about one serious disease. It is not a verdict on creatine for
strength, and a beginner could easily read it as one.

**Proposed wording:** _Creatine looked promising for motor neurone disease in mice. In 175 people
with that disease it made no difference to survival. That is a lesson about animals and people, not
about creatine and strength._

**Why change it:** the current sentence uses three pieces of jargon in eleven words — "animal-to-
human translation", "transgenic", and an unexplained abbreviation — and never says what the disease
is. It is the single hardest sentence on the page for the target reader.

## 5. What is sold, and whether the form matters

**The page says:** a recorded note compares this form with the others that are sold, and the note
itself is kept below, word for word.

**The recorded note says:** Monohydrate is the form used in essentially all of the trial literature
cited here; the alternative salts and esters marketed as superior have not reproduced this evidence
base and in several cases were tested against monohydrate and did not beat it.

**Where it comes from:** the curated record's note on how creatine is sold.

**Kind of evidence:** a summary of which form the trials used. Not a result in itself.

**Who was studied:** not applicable.

**What it cannot settle:** it does not say the other forms are worse, only that they have not shown
the same evidence.

**Proposed wording, as two sentences:** _Almost every trial on this page used creatine monohydrate.
Other forms are sold as better, but they have not reproduced this evidence, and several did not beat
monohydrate when tested against it._

**Why change it:** 41 words in one sentence, with a semicolon. The house limit is 30, and the
meaning survives the split unchanged.

## 6. Why the Tribulus mix-up is on the page

**The page says:** Tribulus terrestris is a plant, not a name of creatine. The full explanation sits
one click below.

**The full explanation says** that an automated name lookup returned a product record whose list of
alternative names contained the plant name, that a merge rule then folded the plant into the
creatine page, and that five trials of the plant were carried onto creatine's record through it.

**Where it comes from:** the correction ledger. It is the record of a mistake RNAWiki found and
fixed.

**Kind of evidence:** an audit record, not medical evidence.

**What it cannot settle:** nothing medical. It exists so a reader can see the page was wrong and is
now right.

**Proposed wording for the short version:** _Tribulus terrestris is a plant, not another name for
creatine. An automated name match wrongly joined them, and five of the plant's trials were counted
on this page until this correction removed them._

**Why change it:** the short version currently stops at "not a name of creatine", which tells a
reader there was a mistake but not what it did to the page.

## The two first-read answers that stopped working

Both of these were written by a person and approved by a reviewer against an exact snapshot of the
record. When the record changed, the approval stopped applying, and the page fell back to recorded
wording. **This is the system working as intended.** An approval covers one version of the evidence,
not the sentence on its own.

Two of the four pages are affected. Semaglutide and inclisiran still hold.

### Creatine

**Approved wording, no longer in use:**

- What it is for: _Used to improve strength and power during short, hard efforts._
- What studies found: _Studies over several decades show that creatine can improve power during
  repeated short, hard efforts._
- The biggest limit: _Two large studies found no evidence that creatine slowed Parkinson's or
  Huntington's disease._

**Why it stopped applying:** this rebuild changed creatine's record twice. It removed a plant's five
trials that had been wrongly attached to the page, and then removed four withdrawn studies that were
still being counted in two stored lists. Both changes were corrections, and both moved the record
the approval was signed against.

**What a reviewer needs to do:** read the three sentences above against the page as it is now and
re-approve them, change them, or replace them. The evidence behind them did not change. What changed
is that the page no longer counts trials of a plant as trials of creatine.

**One thing to check while re-approving:** the third sentence names two diseases and calls the
studies "large" without saying how large. The trials were 1,741 people with Parkinson's disease and
553 with Huntington's disease, and both were stopped early for lack of effect. That is a stronger and
clearer statement than "found no evidence".

### Metformin

**Approved wording, no longer in use:**

- What it is for: _Used to lower blood sugar in type 2 diabetes._
- What studies found: _In one long study of overweight people, metformin reduced deaths compared
  with diet advice alone._
- The biggest limit: _That survival result has not been directly repeated and does not prove that
  metformin slows ageing._

**Why it stopped applying:** metformin's record also moved during this rebuild. The sentences were
not edited; the record they were approved against was.

**What a reviewer needs to do:** the same. Read these three against the current page and re-approve
or revise.

---

## What a reviewer does not need to look at

The registry counts, the trial roles, the source links and the identity checks on this page are
produced by fixed rules from stored records, and they are checked by tests. They do not need a
person's judgement unless one of them looks wrong on the page, in which case it is a defect to
report rather than a sentence to approve.

## Until this list is worked through

The creatine page is honest but thin. Every conclusion on it carries a label saying no reviewer has
signed it off. That is the correct state for an unreviewed record, and it is also the reason the page
is not yet worth showing to readers as a finished thing.
